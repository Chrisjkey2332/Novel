# Chapter 3: Among the Cursed

Soraya felt the horses before she heard them — two of them, out on the dead plain in the dark, their hooves coming up through the rock and into the soles of her bare feet as a small doubled trembling, patient and tired, the walk of animals that had been going a long time and meant to go a while yet. She was sitting on the eastern lip of the mesa with her feet hanging over the drop, the way she was not supposed to, the way she had always done, and she felt them come, and something in her went tight and small and old.

Men on horses, at night, from the direction of the kingdom. She did not have to wonder what they were. She never had to wonder. They came the way weather came, the way winter came, and you did not wonder at winter; you only counted your stores and pulled the little ones close and got ready to lose whatever the cold decided to take.

She should have gone to her father. She went down the path instead, alone, into the dark, because she wanted — and she let herself have this small ugly wanting because no one could see it — she wanted to look at the one they had sent before he could arrange his face into whatever a kingdom arranged its face into when it came to move them along.

He was not what she expected, and she hated, a little, that she noticed.

She had expected fear. They mostly came afraid, the crown's men, under all the straightness they made themselves stand in — she could smell it on them, a sourness the ground carried up to her, the particular reek of a man who has been told he is walking toward monsters and has believed it. This one did not smell afraid. He smelled of horse and cold iron and three days without a fire, and under that, faint, of nothing much at all, a man who had folded himself up so small and so long ago that even his fear had gone quiet. He kept his hands open. He did not reach for the sword, not even the flinch of reaching, not even when she came out of the rock a hand's breadth from him and let him see what she was. He only inclined his head, that small exact tilt, and named himself, and waited to be spoken to.

She did not like it. She could have managed a frightened man, or a cruel one, or a proud one; she knew the shapes of those and how to stand so they broke against her. She did not know the shape of a man who came into her dark and simply, quietly, looked.

So she was unkind to him, because unkindness was a wall and she wanted a wall, and she led him up the path and did not soften it, and told herself she felt nothing but the old tiredness, and mostly that was even true.

---

The camp took him in the way the camp took in weather, folding around the strangeness of him and going on. That was what the kingdoms never understood about her people, and she had stopped trying to explain it: they were not waiting to be saved. They were not even, most of them, angry anymore. They had a supper to cook and children to put down and a hundred small warm stubborn tendernesses to see to before the dark got cold, and a man from the crown was only one more hard fact to be folded in among the others and carried.

She walked him through it and watched him see it, and against her will she watched him seeing.

Vesh was at her fire with the little ones, the blue of her old scales gone soft as dusk-water, a child's torn coat spread across her knees and her needle going and her voice going under it, low, one of the old songs that were really only lists — the names of rivers they would never drink from again, the names of hills gone to sand. Vesh looked up as they passed and met the human's eye and did not look away, and there was no fear in her either, only the long mild measuring warmth she turned on everything, wolves and winters and kings alike, as if she had already decided to be kind to him and was only waiting for him to give her the excuse. Soraya loved her so much in that moment, so suddenly and so helplessly, that she had to look at the ground.

And there — because of course she was there, she was always where the strangeness was — sat Sela. Small and silver and too still, her child's scales bright as a coin in moonlight, watching the human come with those eyes that saw what a child's eyes should not — the not-yet, the coming, the griefs no one so small should have to hold. The other children had drifted from her the way they always did, a little space opening around the spirit-born the way water opens around a stone, and Sela sat in her little ring of apartness and did not seem to mind, or had learned so early to seem not to mind that it came to the same. When the human passed her she went stiller still, and her small mouth opened, and Soraya's heart lurched up into her throat — not now, little one, not out loud, not in front of him — but the child only breathed, and watched, and let him go by, and turned her strange bright eyes up to Soraya instead with something in them that had no name, that Soraya did not want to name, that she buried under the next breath and the next step and did not carry any further.

Ravan was by the forge-fire, and Ravan was watching her, not the human. He was always watching her. His scales threw the flame back red and gold, brilliant, beautiful, the strong clean colour of what she was supposed to have wanted, and he stood there in all that easy fire and watched her walk a human through the camp and she felt his eyes on the side of her face like heat off a stone. She did not look at him. She had stopped looking at him a long time ago, on purpose, and it had cost her something she did not let herself add up, and she was not going to add it up tonight of all nights with a crown's man at her shoulder counting her people like coins.

Because that was what he was doing. She could see it. He walked through her fold with his open hands and his quiet folded face and he counted — the wagons, the tents, the cattle, the children — and she wanted to hate him for it, and reached for the hate the way she reached for the wall, and found, underneath where the hate should have been, only the same tiredness, and something worse than tiredness that she shoved down hard and did not look at.

---

She stood at her father's shoulder through the parley and said nothing, because it was not hers to say yet, and because if she opened her mouth too soon she did not entirely trust what would come out of it.

Her father was so tired. She could see it in the banked dull red of him, the fire he had spent a hundred years learning to hold down until the holding was most of what he was. He said the words he always said, the worn smooth words, and the human said the words they always said, the law and the reason and the king who bore them no ill will, and it went along its old groove toward its old ending, and Soraya let the ache of it sit in her chest, the ache that came every time, the one she never named — that they would fold their tents again, that Vesh would sing the list of another lost place, that the ground under Sela's small feet would go to ash because they had let themselves rest on it, that this was her whole inheritance and her whole future, to lead this folding, again and again, until the road or the dark took her the way it took everything soft enough to be taken.

And then the human broke the groove.

He agreed with her father. No — he agreed with *her*, with what she had thrown at him from the edge of the fire, the accusation she had not been able to keep behind her teeth after all: *do not call it the weather.* And instead of the smooth denial, the patient crown's-man reasonableness she had braced against, he had gone still and looked at her the way he had looked at her in the dark on the path, and said *you are right.* He had said he did not believe the tales. He had called her people two innocent peoples caught on one frontier. He had called them souls — not in the word, but under it, in the way he had stopped counting them and started, for a moment, seeing them, and she had watched it happen on his folded quiet face and had not known what to do with the watching.

It undid something in her that she needed to keep done up, and she was furious with him for it, a clean bright fury she was grateful for because it was so much simpler than everything underneath.

Her father promised him a price in the morning, and closed his eyes, and was old. And because her father was old and had spent his fire holding the fold together for longer than the human's whole kingdom had stood, Soraya did the one thing she could still do for him: she took the stranger off his hands. She jerked her chin at the human and turned and did not check to see if he followed, and led him out toward the far tents, into the dark, away from her father and her fire and the child who saw too much.

---

Later, when the human was bedded down at the cold edge of the camp with the boy who came with him, her father sent for her, and she went and sat on the ground by his chair the way she had sat since she was small, and for a while neither of them said anything and the fire talked to itself between them.

"You met him on the path," her father said finally. Not a question. He knew her.

"I wanted to see him first."

"And?"

She looked into the coals. She could have told him what she had reasoned, if she had reasoned anything — but she had not reasoned; she had only felt, and the feeling sat in her like a swallowed stone, and she did not know how to hand it to him except plainly. "He is not like the others."

"No," her father agreed, and there was something in the word she did not like, something that had been awake at the fire and was awake still. "He is not. That is the trouble with this one. The cruel ones and the frightened ones move you along and are gone, and you can hate them cleanly and forget them by the next moon. The honest ones—" He stopped. His banked eyes found her in the firelight, and held her, and she felt suddenly and horribly like the child she had been, the one who had wanted a home so badly it had killed a home. "The honest ones stay with you. Be careful of the honest ones, Soraya. They cost the most, in the end, and they never mean to, and that is exactly why."

She did not ask him what he meant. She was afraid she already knew, in the wordless underneath place where she knew things before she let herself, and she stood, and kissed the rough warm crown of her father's head the way she had when she was small and he had been made of fire, and went out into the camp to walk it until the wanting quieted, the way she always did, the way she always had to, every single night of her life.

The ground was dying under her feet as she walked. She could feel it going, the slow surrender in the soil, the green giving up from the roots — because they were here, because they had stayed a season, because that was what she was, a thing the earth could not keep. She crouched at the camp's edge where no one would see and pressed her palm flat to the cold grey of it anyway, helplessly, the way she always did, and she coaxed, and a single blade came, then three, cool and trembling and green and already dying against her fingers.

She let them come. She always let them come.

And she did not think about the human, bedded down out there in the dark with his open hands and his quiet folded face and his terrible honest eyes, she did not think about him at all, and the not-thinking took her whole heart to manage, and the little green blades curled up cool against her skin and died, and above her the stars were very far, and somewhere behind her Sela was awake in the dark and watching, the way the child watched everything that was coming, and could not stop.
