# Chapter 2: The Standoff

They crossed the waste in the dark as the knight would not make camp in it.

Wat had asked, once, in a small voice, whether they might stop. The horses were uneasy, the ash got into everything, into the mouth and the eyes and the folds of a man's clothes, and the cold came up out of the dead ground like breath from a cellar. Ser Warrant had said no with no explanation given, and the boy had not asked again. The truth, which the knight kept to himself as he kept most truths, was that he did not care to sleep on land that was in the act of dying. A battlefield he could sleep on. A battlefield was over; whatever had happened there was finished and a man could lie down in the aftermath and take what rest the dead allowed. This was different. This was still going on, slowly, all around them in the dark. At times he felt he could hear it, a faint continual settling, the sound of a place giving up, and some animal wisdom under his caution insisted that a man who lay down in it might wake to find that some measure of the slowness had reached him too.

So they rode, and the grey mare picked her way by the light of a thin moon, and the ash muffled the fall of the hooves until they seemed to travel in a silence of their own making, two riders and two horses moving through a country that had stopped answering. Once Wat began, from nerves, to talk some bright brave nonsense about the stars, about a girl in the lower town, about anything at all, and the knight let him. The sound of a living voice was its own small defiance out here and although frightened, the boy had earned the mercy of pretending he was not.

The Kessith mesa grew against the stars ahead of them until it blotted out a third of the sky.

It was bigger than it had looked from the rise. A pale red stone, sheer on the near face, its flat crown standing perhaps two hundred feet above the plain. Small, warm, and stubborn, the orange stitch of fires dotted the crown of the stone. People were up there cooking their suppers, banking their coals, and putting their children to bed on a table of rock in the middle of a country they were killing. The ordinariness of it, the sheer domestic patience of it, was the first note that did not fit the song he had been sent to sing.

They found the path at the mesa's shoulder, a switchback cut by no human hand, too regular for weather and too old for any mason of Marrenhold; the steps of it were worn shallow in the centres, the way stone wears under the passage of a great many feet over a great many years. A people cursed to wander did not, one would think, wear grooves in stone, unless the wearing had been done long ago, in some place they had once believed was home. He put the thought aside to examine later. Halfway up, the sentries found them.

The knight had expected to be challenged. He had not expected the challenger.

It came out of the rock itself, a shape unfolding from a shadow that his eye had read as stone, and then standing in the path above them with the unhurried certainty of a creature unfamiliar with haste. Tall. Taller than the tallest man the knight had ever seen. In the moonlight its skin held a dull gleam that resolved, as it came a step nearer, into scales, each one small and close-set across the cheekbones and the backs of the hands, the colour of old riverbed, brown and dun with a banked orange beneath, as if there were a coal somewhere under them that had been let go cool. Its eyes caught the moon and threw it back, gold, the pupils drawn to slots. And at its shoulders, folded and bound and unmistakable for anything else a body might carry, were wings.

He had never seen one before. No Marrenholder of his generation had; the Emberkin were a tale told to frighten children, not a creature a man expected to meet on a dark path. And so for a long moment his mind would not give it a name, or gave it only the wrong ones. His eye reported not a person but an entity out of the old stories, a beast that had learned to stand upright, and every instinct trained into him across a hard life told him to read it as danger, as quarry, as other, and to reach for the sword. He knew the reflex for what it was. He did not trust it. He did not, in that first moment, entirely master it either. It is one thing to ride three days telling yourself the monsters are only people. It is another to stand in the dark before the first of them and make the telling hold.

Wat made a sound. Not a word. The knight did not look at him. He kept his hands loose and open and away from the sword, and he inclined his head the precise degree that acknowledged a person without conceding a position, and he waited to be spoken to. He had come to treat and a man who draws first has already failed to do so.

"You are lost," it said. The voice was a woman's, low and even and entirely without welcome, and the plain living grievance in it made the beast his mind had reached for that much harder to hold. "The road you want is the one that goes back."

"I am not lost," said the knight. "I am Ser Warrant, a guarantor of the crown of Marrenhold, and I have come to speak with whoever speaks for your people. Tonight, if they will hear me."

Something moved in the gold eyes; not surprise, but the cold, unhurried filing-away of a fact. She gazed over him for the length of several breaths, and then past him at Wat, who was doing his valiant sixteen-year-old best not to stare; failing at it wholesale. Her gaze then wandered to the two tired horses, and then back at the knight. The knight had the uncomfortable sense of having been counted, weighed, and priced, all in the space it took the moon to clear a rag of cloud. He was accustomed to being the one who understood the room. It was a novelty, and not entirely a welcome one, to be so plainly and quickly read by someone standing on the other side of a dark path.

"Guarantor," she said, and she weighed the word on her tongue like a coin she suspected of being false. "The crown sends its honest man to move us along. How courteous of it, to send the honest one. The last three sent their cruel ones, and one before that sent a frightened one, which was the worst. A frightened man will do a cruel deed quickly to be gone." She turned without waiting for an answer, her bound wings shifting once against her back with a dry whisper of membrane. "Leave the horses. Follow. Do not touch anyone, and do not put your hand to that sword, guarantor, or you will learn how quickly courtesy ends."

The knight and the boy shared a glance, then did as they were bid, leaving the horses and following her up the path.

---

The camp on the crown of the mesa was not what the knight had spent three days building in his mind, and he made himself notice every particular of how it was not.

He had imagined a horde of uncivilized wandering beasts scattering about in disarray, taking from one another just as they took life from the land itself. What he found was a caravan at rest. Wagons had been drawn into a rough ring, hide tents pitched in the lee of them, cook-fires tended and small and shielded from the wind with stones, a picket of lean cattle and a few goats, and everywhere the quiet ordered motion of a group who had struck and made this same camp ten thousand times before. There were perhaps three hundred of them. He revised that down as he walked: three hundred counting the wagons that would be stores, the tents that would stand empty, the spaces that had once held people who were no longer there to fill them. Two hundred, then. Fewer. A people, not a horde. A people, and not a large one. And not a rich one, either: he saw it in the careful way they portioned the cook-pots, in the mending upon mending upon mending of the children's coats. A people at the frayed end of a very long rope.

They were all of them scaled, and the scales were not all one colour. That was the second particular, and it stopped him for half a step before his discipline caught him and moved him on. By a fire to his left sat an old woman whose scales were the soft blue of deep water, mending a child's coat from scraps of what appeared to be her own; the child beside her was the same blue, brighter, almost luminous, and watched the knight pass with the frank unblinking interest of the very young. Farther on, a knot of men banked a forge-fire, and their scales ran red and gold and threw the firelight back so that they seemed lit from within, as though the fire they tended were only the outward echo of one they carried. Here and there a paler grey, a wan near-white, quiet folk who kept to the edges. And his guide, and here and there another, the brown-and-orange of turned earth. He did not understand the colours. He understood only that they meant something, that they were read among these people the way a herald's tabard is read, and that he had been given no key to the reading himself.

And still, under the noticing, the old wrongness worked at him. It would have been easier to think of them as animals. The scales invited it; the slotted eyes invited it; some cowardly, comfortable corner of his mind kept trying to file the whole camp under beast and have done, because a beast could be moved off good ground without any weight on a man's conscience. He refused it, and the refusing was work, and the work did not wholly take. *Person*, he told himself, watching a scaled mother hush a scaled child against her shoulder. But the word would not seat itself. It slid off the scales and the slotted eyes and the bound wings and found no purchase, and he understood that whatever these were, his mind had built no shelf yet to set them on, and would not build one in an evening. He had ridden three days telling himself the monsters were only people. It was going to take a great deal longer than three days, and a great deal more than the telling, before the believing followed.

The third particular was the one he would remember longest, because it was the one that broke the story for good.

None of them were armed.

Not none. That was not quite true, and the knight did not permit himself the comfort of the round untruth. There were spears stacked by the wagons, and here and there a long knife at a belt, the tools of a people who lived on a road that ran through wolf country. But no one *wore* war. No one among two hundred souls stood in the posture of a fighter minding a stranger in their camp: the hand drifting near the hilt, the weight settling onto the balls of the feet, the small unresting readiness that a soldier cannot put down even when he no longer lives his life in war and the battlefield is far behind him, even in his own hall, and not even while he is asleep. The knight had spent his life among men who could not put it down. He wore it himself; he could feel it in his own shoulders now, the old habit of a body that had learned early the world was not safe. He knew its absence the way a man raised by the sea knows the silence of the mountains. These people watched him, warily, with no love at all, but they watched him the way herdsmen watch a wolf that has wandered into the fold in daylight. In their eyes he alone is a danger to be managed, not a rival to be met.

When his guide led him past a wagon a young man rose too fast from beside it, alarmed, and for half an instant seemed about to move on the knight. The young man's shoulders came up and his bound wings flared, mantling forward, a wall of scaled membrane thrown between his body and the stranger. A shield. Not a blade. His whole startled instinct, in the sudden presence of a threat, had been to cover, not to strike. Someone murmured to him, and he subsided, and folded his wings. The look of shame smeared across the young lizard's face in a way the knight recognized and could not at once place. Within its eyes were the shame of a man who had flinched when danger approached, among a people for whom flinching is the only strength left.

The knight had come to move monsters off good ground. In this moment as he looked about the camp and took into account that which ran counter to his understanding. At least for the moment, there were no monsters here.

---

The once-stone lizard brought the knight and his aide to the center of the ring, where the largest fire burned, and where an old man sat waiting for him as though he had been waiting a long while; not for the knight in particular, but for something like him, wearing his authority; for the next in a long line of men who had come to say the next in a long line of the same words.

He was old the way the mountains were old. His scales were the red and gold of the forge-fire men, but where theirs blazed his had gone to the deep dull colour of a coal with the once fierce flame all but gone out of it, banked under grey ash, and the knight, who had learned to read the colours not at all, read that much without being taught: here was a fire that had been very great once, and had been made to burn low at some unknown cost. Heavy horns swept back from his brow, notched and worn. His wings, bound like all the rest, were vast; folded, they still stood higher than his head. He did not rise. Often a leader who does not rise to receive the envoy of a king is either insulting you or has nothing left to prove, and the knight, watching the weariness linger on the shoulders of the old lizard as a great weight, judged it was not an insult.

At the old man's right hand stood the sentry who had brought them up. So. Not a sentry. Or not only.

"Sit, guarantor," the old man said. His voice was low and worn smooth, a riverstone of a voice. "You have crossed the land in the dark, which tells me you did not wish to sleep in it. You have some sense, which is more than the last one had. Sit, and say the words you were sent to say, and we will all pretend for a little while that saying them is not the whole of what will happen here."

The knight sat. Wat folded down beside him, too close, and the knight let him.

"You know why I have come," the knight said.

"I have known why men like you come since before your grandfather's grandfather drew breath," the old man said, without heat, without even much interest, a statement of the weather. "You have come to tell me to move my people off ground the crown of Marrenhold has decided it wants. You will dress it in law, because your kingdom loves the law, and the law will be true, and it will make no difference at all to the truth underneath it, which is that we are here and you would rather we were not." He turned his gold eyes on the knight, and for the first time there was something in them beyond weariness: a flicker, quickly banked, like the coal under the ash remembering for an instant what it was. "Say it anyway. You crossed the dark to say it and I would not cheat you of it."

This was not, the knight reflected, how these conversations usually began. Usually he was the one who understood the shape of the room and everyone else who was catching up. He set that discomfort aside and resolved himself to the work he had come to do.

"The land is dying where you camp," he said. "You know this. You are standing in the middle of it. In a season more it will reach the wells that a great many people who are not you depend on to live. My king has no quarrel with your people and no wish to spill your blood. Of these things both are true and I will hold to them, but you cannot stay on the Kessith frontier. That is not a threat and it is not a judgment. The ground itself will not hold you and Marrenhold cannot spare it. I have come to ask you to go, and to see it done lawfully and without harm."

He had said it plainly, and he had meant every word, and he watched it land on the old man and saw it do nothing at all, not because the old lizard rejected it, but because he had heard it so many times, in so many mouths, in so many valleys and passes and river-bends, that it had worn a groove in him that the phrases simply moved along.

The woman spoke.

The knight had almost forgotten her, the knight did not usually forget the armed person standing at the edge of a parley, and she was the most dangerous person at this fire. Her scales were the brown and orange of turned earth, but they did not carry the same dullness as the old lizard. They were the brightest colour the knight had seen in the whole camp thus far; vivid, banked with a light his eye wanted to call heat and his sense knew was not. If the colours meant anything then this one was very much that thing, and had chosen to stand behind an old man's chair in silence until now.

"You speak of harm," she said, "as if you were not the harm. You come with your law and your clean hands and your king who has no quarrel with us, and you ask us, so reasonably, to go as others have done before you." Her voice did not rise, but remained as steady as the stone of the mesa. "If requested we will go, as we always do, but do not sit at our fire, guarantor, and tell us the ground is the only one turning us out. We know exactly what turns us out. Do not insult our people by simply calling it the weather."

The knight looked at her for a moment. Then he did the one thing he had learned in eleven years of rooms like this one, that almost no one in his position ever did, because it felt like weakness and was in fact the only strength that ever changed anything.

He agreed with her.

"You are right," he said. "It is not the weather. I do not know what it is. I have been told tales that I did not believe until now that the earth where your people wander withers beneath your very feet. While I could aim to speculate as to how this happens I would rather hear the truth from someone who carries it than repeat a story I was handed others who may not know. Whatever the source of your malady is, it is doing to that plain out there what I saw with my own eyes on the ride in. My king's people will die of disease if it reaches their wells or will suffer a more horific fate if it reaches their crop, and they are no more guilty of your grief than you are of their thirst or hunger. While we may stand as two innocent peoples, this land cannot hold them both. I did not make it so. I am only the one chosen to stand in the middle." Ser Warrant lets the words hand in the air for a moment before continuing. "You say you always go. Then we are most of the way to agreement already, and only haggling over the terms of it. So. What would it cost to have you gone whole, and gone willing, with no children of yours or mine buried over it?"

Something changed in the old man's face. Not softening. The knight would not have insulted him by imagining he had softened him in the space of a single fire. But the weariness lifted a hand's breadth, and under it was something sharper and more awake.

He was quiet for a while. The fire spoke to itself in soft murmurs of crackling wood. Somewhere behind the wagons a child laughed, once, at something, and was hushed, and the knight found that the small sound moved him in a way the whole grey ruin of the plain had not.

"There is a cost," the old man said at last, slowly, as though setting down each word to be sure of its weight before he trusted the next one on top of it. "There is always a cost, guarantor, and it is never the one your kind expects, and it is never one your kind finds easy to pay, because your kind measures the world in coin and the price is not in coin." He lifted his banked-gold gaze, and held the knight's, and the coal under the ash was awake now and looking out. "You crossed the dark to spend no blood. Good. Cross a little further with me, and we will see whether your king meant a word of the fine speech he sent up my mesa in your mouth. We will see what an honest man is worth, when the honesty costs him something."

"Name it," said the knight.

"In the morning," said the old man, and closed his eyes, and was, abruptly, only old again. "It is a matter to be said in daylight. You have had a long ride and I have lived a long three centuries that now wears on my bones. Soraya will find you somewhere to sleep that is not on the dying ground." The faintest dry ghost of what might once have been humour. "You will find we are better hosts than you may have expected. It is one of the few luxuries left to a people who own nothing: we can afford to be kind, because kindness is the one possession the road has never yet found a way to take."

The woman with the earth-bright scales (Soraya, the old man had called her) did not look as though kindness was anywhere in her plans. She looked at the knight the way a mason looks at a wall he has been told to build and has already found flaws within the design, and she jerked her chin toward the dark edge of the camp, and turned wordlessly with the expectation to be followed.

The knight rose. He had come to deliver a demand and have it resisted, and instead he had been promised a price he could not yet see, by a man he had come prepared to despise and found he could not, in front of a people he had come to move like cattle and found he could not. It was, he thought, following the straight cold back of the woman into the dark, a poor beginning to a simple task.

He was right about that, though not in any of the ways he yet imagined. He would think of this night often, later, when there was little else left to think on. This first night, when the whole ruin was still only a demand unspoken and a price unnamed, and he might still, had he been a lesser man or a wiser one, have simply gathered the boy and the horses and ridden back down into the honest dark and gone home.

He did not. He followed her. That was the beginning of it, though he would not have called it a beginning; he would have called it his duty, and he would have been right, and it would not have saved him.
