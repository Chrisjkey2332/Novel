# Chapter 6: The Keeper

The keeping settled into a shape, the way everything did, given a few days and a people who knew how to make a life out of whatever ground they were standing on.

By the fourth day it had a rhythm Soraya could have set a song to. She woke before the camp and found him already awake — he woke as she did, early and complete, no drowse in him, and she told herself it was suspicious and did not believe herself. She walked him through the fold's morning at her heel, close enough to touch, and he made himself as small and dull as he had promised, and it did not work in the slightest, because a man that still and that watchful could no more be dull than a drawn bow could be mistaken for a stick. But he tried, and the trying was its own courtesy, and the fold noticed the trying. By the fourth day the water-girl set his cup down a little less as though she expected it to bite her. By the sixth, a boy dared him to a game of stones and the human lost, twice, and Soraya could not decide whether he had lost on purpose, and then decided that a man who told the truth as a matter of religion would not cheat even at losing, and then was annoyed with herself for the whole line of thought, which had taken up more of her morning than she would ever admit.

He asked for work. She had not believed he meant it, that first afternoon, and had given him the meanest, dullest labour she could find — the mending of harness, hours of it, the cracked and sweated leather of a people too poor to replace what could still be stitched — half to be rid of him and half to see how long the crown's man would last at a servant's task before his pride showed. His pride did not show. He sat with the harness across his knees, day on day, at the edge of the fire where she could see him, and he mended it, clumsily at first and then well, his hands learning the awl the way she supposed his hands had learned everything, patiently, without complaint, and he did not once look up to see whether she was watching him be humble, which was how she knew he was not being humble. He was only being useful. There was a difference, and it undid her a little to find he knew it.

The fold began, by degrees she could measure only in retrospect, to let him. Torr the wheelwright, who trusted no one quickly and crowns least of all, found the human's hands steady and started passing him the holding-work, the dull second pair of hands a wheel wants, and the two of them fell into the wordless companionship of men who would rather share a task than a conversation. Old Petras stopped moving his stool when the human sat near the fire. The grey-scaled man who had promised to deal with him himself brought his own torn harness to the human's pile one evening, wordless, and took it back mended the next, and said nothing, and that was, in the grammar of the fold, a whole speech. It was not liking. It was not trust. It was the slow animal recalculation of a wary people who had watched a danger sit among their children for six days and do nothing but stitch their leather as though their children were his own, and had begun, against their every hard-won instinct, to lower the pitch of their fear by a single tone.

Soraya watched it happen and did not know whether to be glad of it or afraid, and settled, as she settled most days, on both.

"You stitch like a saddler now," she said, on the sixth day, because the silence had gone on too long and she wanted to break it and would not examine why. "You did not, at the start."

"No." He turned the mended cheekpiece in the grey light, testing the seam with his thumb. "At the start I did it the way a man does a task he means to be finished with. That leather does not care how I feel about it. It only knows whether the stitch will hold when a beast pulls against it on a bad road with a child in the wagon behind." He set it down on the finished pile, which had grown, over the days, into a small monument to his patience. "So I learned to do it as though the child were mine. It goes slower. It holds better. I have found that of most work."

*As though the child were mine.* She looked at him and did not know what to do with him. She had kept crown's men before, in her mind, in the rehearsals she ran of the leader she would one day have to be, and none of the imagined ones had ever said a plain true thing like that and then simply gone back to their labour, unaware they had said anything at all. She had built her whole defence for a different enemy. This one kept walking around the side of it.

Ravan passed while she was still looking, and saw her looking, and his scales flared once, bright, before he mastered them and moved on without a word — but the word was in the set of his shoulders, and she heard it anyway, *the wall, Soraya, the wall,* and she straightened and hardened her face and told herself he was right. He was right. She turned the hardness on the human like a lamp.

"Do not," she said, "make yourself easy to like. It is the cruelest gift you could give my people, and I think you are clever enough to know it, which makes it worse. They cannot afford to like you. You are the crown, and you will leave, and taking their liking with you when you go is a theft, whatever else your fine oath forbids you." She had not meant to say so much. It had come out of the sore place, unguarded, and she saw it land on him, and saw — to her surprise — that it landed as truth and not as insult, that he received it the way she had received his agreement at the fire, with a small still respect for a hard truth honestly said.

"That is fair," he said, after a moment. "It had not occurred to me, and it should have. I am so accustomed to being feared that I had not weighed the cost of being borne." He considered it with that whole care he brought to everything, as though her small angry truth deserved the same attention he gave a king's charge. "I cannot make myself unlikable to order — a man trying to be disliked is only another performance, and your people would smell it; they smell everything. But I can keep my distance. I can be a pair of hands and not a face. If your fold must lose me in a fortnight, better they lose a tool than a friend. You are right. I will be careful of their hearts." A pause, and then, lower, almost to himself: "I have not often been asked to be careful of anyone's."

She should have left it there. She had won the point; the wall was up; the sensible course was to walk away and let the silence finish the work. But the last words had come out of some uncurtained window again, that same tired unguarded place, and before her sense could stop her the wordless part of her had already answered them, and what it made her say was not cold at all.

"Who are you," she asked, "under the grey?"

He went still.

"You give the crown a name — Ser Warrant — and the fold a shape it can fear, and you hand out your reasons like coin so no one goes looking for the man who holds them. But there is a man who holds them. I have kept you six days. I have watched you be kind to leather for the sake of a child you will never meet. That is not the crown; the crown does not care about the child." She heard her own voice go somewhere she had not sent it. "So. Who is careful of leather, when he thinks no one is counting the man?"

The human was very still now — stiller than his usual stillness, a different quality of it, the stillness of a creature touched somewhere it did not know was still tender. For a long moment she thought he would not answer, would fold the window shut and give her the grey, and she braced for it, and told herself she wanted it, the grey, the wall, the safe cold distance of two enemies keeping a bargain.

He did not give her the grey. But he did not give her the man, either. What he gave her was smaller and stranger and, she understood only much later, far more dangerous than either.

"No one has asked me that," he said slowly, "in a very long time. I am not certain I still have the answer. I gave it up — the answer — a while ago, for reasons that were good then and may still be good; I no longer look at them closely enough to say." He met her eyes, and for once there was nothing careful in it, only a plain bewildered honesty, a man startled into the truth. "You are the first person in eleven years to look past the grey and simply assume there was someone behind it worth the looking. I do not know what to do with that. I am a little ashamed of how much I do not know what to do with it."

They sat with that a while, the two of them, at the edge of the fire, in the grey afternoon, and neither of them looked away, which was its own kind of falling, though neither would have called it that yet.

"My father says I should be careful of you," she said finally, because it was true, and because she had begun, without deciding to, to hand him true things, which was a habit she should not have been forming and could not seem to break. "Not for the fold. For myself. He would not say what he meant. Vesh will not say. Ravan says it and means his own hurt, so I do not count it. But three of the people I trust most have warned me of you in a single week, and you have done nothing in that week but mend harness and lose at stones and refuse to be either the monster or the saint the story wanted. So I will ask you plainly, since plain is the one language you seem to keep: are they right to warn me?"

The human looked at her for a long time. She watched him weigh it — not whether to answer, she understood, but how to answer without lying, which for him was the only shape a question ever came in.

"I do not know what they see," he said at last. "I know what I feel, and I am not in the habit of feeling it, and I have not the practice to name it well. But I will not lie to you, because I do not lie, and because you asked plainly. Yes. I think they are right to warn you. I think—" and here his voice, so level through everything, caught, once, on nothing, the way a wheel catches on a stone too small to see— "I think I am becoming, in this camp, a man I gave up being a long time ago, on purpose, because being him cost more than I could pay. And I do not know how to stop. And I am not sure, God forgive me, that I want to. That is the whole of the truth, and it is more than you asked, and more than I have said aloud to anyone in eleven years, and I do not know why I have said it to you except that you looked at me as though there were someone here to say it, and it appears there is, and he has been waiting a very long time to be spoken to."

Soraya, who had asked the question to test him, to find the seam in him, to arm herself — Soraya stood in the cold grey afternoon with her carefully cold terms all delivered and her wall all built, and felt the ground shift again, that slow deep movement under the surface that no one else could feel, and knew, in the wordless place, that she had not found his seam at all.

She had found her own.

She turned and left him with the harness and did not trust herself to say goodnight, and walked out to the camp's edge, and crouched, and pressed her palm to the dying ground, and the green came, cool and trembling, and she held it and did not weep, by simply deciding not to, over and over, the way she had learned. And she thought, with the clean cold flare of the fury she was so grateful for: *my father knew. He knew exactly what he was doing when he set me to keep the honest one. Be careful of him for yourself, he said. He did not mean the danger. He meant this.*

And she was angry at her father, and she loved him, and she was afraid, all at once, the way she had been her whole life; and none of it, not one grain of it, made her want to stop.

That was the part she could not forgive herself. Not the wanting. The not wanting to stop.
