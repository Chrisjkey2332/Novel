# Chapter 1: The Charge

The throne room of Marrenhold had been carved, three centuries past, from the living rock of the pass, and no fire had ever quite warmed it. Ser Warrant had stood in colder places. He kept his hands loose at his sides and his eyes on the king, and he waited, as he had been taught to wait, without seeming to.

King Rowan was reading. He read the way he did most things, slowly, with the whole of his attention, as though the parchment in his hands might be the last honest document in the world and he meant to give it its due. The lamps had been lit against the mountain dark that came early to the pass, and the light moved on the old man's face and found every year of it. He was not truly old — not yet sixty — but the crown of Marrenhold aged a man the way salt aged iron, from the inside, invisibly, until one day the thing simply gave.

"They are dying," Rowan said at last, not looking up. "The frontier. Did you see it, riding in?"

"I saw it, my king."

"Tell me."

Ser Warrant had learned long ago that when the king said *tell me* he did not want a report. He wanted the thing itself, laid down plainly, so that he could stand where you had stood and see with your eyes. It was, the knight had come to understand, a kind of humility that looked like command.

"The barley is gone in the eastern fields," he said. "Not burned. Not blighted in the way of a wet year. It has simply — stopped. The stalks are grey. They break like ash between the fingers and leave no green. The wells past the old mill are turning bitter. The Harrow folk have pulled back to the second ridge, and they have taken their dead with them, which is how I knew it was bad, because the Harrow folk do not move their dead for anything short of the end of the world."

Rowan set the parchment down. "And the cause of it sits on the Kessith mesa, and will not go."

"The Emberkin," said the knight.

"The Emberkin." The king said the word without heat, the way he said all difficult words, turning it over to be sure of it. "A wandering people, cursed — so the tales run — never to keep a home; and wherever they keep one anyway, the land dies to be rid of them. They came to the mesa a season ago. In a season more there will be no eastern frontier. In two, the bitter will reach the town wells, and then Marrenhold will be a kingdom of stone and thirst wedged between two empires that have wanted an excuse to swallow it for a hundred years." He folded his hands. "I would like them to go, Warrant. I would like it very much."

"Then they will go," the knight said.

It was not arrogance. He had said it because it was true in the way that his word was always true — a thing he could make true by the saying of it, because the saying bound him. That was what he was. Somewhere south, the Vharr Empire settled its endless quarrels of rank by the duel, and somewhere north the Niven Empire settled theirs by the whisper and the ledger, and between them little Marrenhold survived on a single commodity that neither empire could counterfeit: a word that could be trusted. The kingdom's guarantors were that word made flesh. When a guarantor swore, the oath was not a promise. It was a binding, sealed in the faith of the Father, the Mother, and the Child, and to break it was to be unmade — to have the thing that the oath had given you taken back, down to the roots.

Ser Warrant knew exactly what the oath had given him. He did not think of it. He had trained himself not to, the way a man learns not to touch a scar.

"Sit," Rowan said. "You have been riding for three days and standing for one hour, and it offends me."

The knight sat. There was a second chair by the king's, plain, worn smooth on the arms; it had been the knight's chair for eleven years, since the day he had first been permitted to sit in a king's presence without being commanded twice. He had never grown easy in it. He suspected he never would, and he suspected further that this was why Rowan kept it there — to remind them both, gently, of the distance the knight had come and the distance he still, in his own mind, had not.

"How is the boy?" the king asked.

"Devoted. Reckless. Certain the world is fair and that I am the proof of it." The knight allowed himself the ghost of something that was not quite a smile. "He will make a fine guarantor, in ten years, if he lives, and if the world does not teach him otherwise first."

"The world always does," Rowan said. "That is what the ten years are for." He looked at the knight then, full on, and there was in his face the thing that the knight had spent eleven years failing to deserve and failing to escape — a plain, unhurried love, the love of a man for something he had made with his own hands and found, to his own quiet astonishment, to be good. "I did not send for you only for the Emberkin. You know that."

"I suspected it, my king."

"The empires are moving." Rowan's voice did not change, but the room seemed to lean closer to hear it. "The Vharr have been mustering their tribes since the thaw — duels called, ranks settled, the pecking-order of a whole continent being fixed in blood, which they only ever do before a war. And the Niven have gone quiet in the way that the Niven go quiet, which is worse than any mustering, because it means the ledgers are being balanced and the whispers are being spent. Marrenhold sits in the one pass that joins their two hungers. When they decide to feed on one another, they will want the pass, and they will want it clean, and a kingdom that keeps its word to both sides is the last thing either side desires." He drew a slow breath. "It is coming, Warrant. Not this month. Perhaps not this year. But it is coming, and when it comes I will need every guarantor I have standing on the right ground at the right hour, or there will be no Marrenhold to guarantee."

"I understand."

"I do not think you do, yet, but you will." The king pushed the parchment across the table. It bore the sigil of the crown pressed in grey wax, and beneath it, in Rowan's own careful hand, the terms of a charge. "So. The Emberkin first, because they are the wound I can staunch this season, and because a king who cannot move a wandering camp off his own frontier will not be trusted to move an empire off his own throne. Go to the Kessith mesa. Treat with their leader. Move them along — humanely, lawfully, and without a drop of blood if it can be helped, because the whole point of us, Warrant, the entire and only point, is that we do not spill what we can spare." He paused. "But move them. And then come home. I will have need of you before the leaves turn."

The knight took the charge. The wax was still faintly warm. "By what authority do I treat?"

"By the crown's, and your own." Which meant: as a guarantor. Which meant that whatever he swore on the mesa, Marrenhold would keep, though it cost the treasury or the crown or the king his sleep — because if it did not, the knight would be unmade for the lie, and Rowan of Marrenhold did not spend his men that way. It was the most dangerous power the small kingdom possessed, and it rested, at present, in the hands of a man who had been born owning nothing, not even his own name.

"It will be done," said Ser Warrant, and rose, and bowed, and did not say the rest of it — that he would rather have stayed, that the road out of the pass in autumn was a hard one, that some part of him that he did not examine had hoped, absurdly, that the king had sent for him only to sit by the fire and be looked at like something worth having made. He had abandoned the habit of hoping for such things a long time ago. He was only sometimes surprised to find the habit had not entirely abandoned him.

At the door the king spoke again, quietly, so that the guards would not hear.

"Tomas."

The knight went still. There were perhaps three people living who knew the name, and only one who was permitted to use it, and even he used it rarely, as one spends a coin too dear to break.

"Come back to me," Rowan said. "That is not in the charge. That is a separate thing, and I am asking it as a separate thing. Do you hear?"

"I hear you," said the knight, and could not, for a moment, say anything else. Then he went out, and became Ser Warrant again in the cold corridor, the way a man puts on a wet cloak, and did not let himself feel the weight of it settle until he was well down the stair.

---

Wat had the horses saddled before dawn, which meant Wat had not slept, which meant Wat had spent the night doing the small unnecessary excellent things that boys do when they love someone and have no other way to say so. The girths were checked twice. The waterskins were full and the spare had been oiled at the seam. Someone had worked a burr out of the grey mare's tail with a comb and, from the look of the boy's fingers, a good deal of patience and at least one bite.

"You didn't sleep," the knight said.

"I slept, Ser." Wat was sixteen, or near enough that the difference was a matter of pride rather than fact, and he lied the way sixteen lies, badly and with total conviction. "A little. Enough."

"Mm." The knight ran his hand down the mare's neck. She leaned into it. Animals had always trusted him more readily than people did, and he had never decided whether that was a comfort or an indictment. "You know where we are going."

"The Kessith mesa. The Emberkin." The boy's eyes were bright in the torchlight. He had a good face, open as a field, the kind of face that had never once had to hide anything and so had never learned how, and the knight felt the old ache of it, the wish that were half tenderness and half warning: *stay like that, and it will break you; do not stay like that, and it will be a different kind of breaking.* "Is it true, what they say? That they've got wings? That the ground dies where they walk?"

"Some of it will be true. Most of what people say about a thing they fear is a way of not looking at it." He swung up into the saddle. "We will look at it. That is the whole of our craft, Wat, when you strip the oaths and the sigils off it. We go and we look at the thing itself, and then we tell the truth about what we saw, and we keep the word we gave. Everything else is decoration."

"Yes, Ser." The boy mounted, less gracefully, and gathered his reins, and then, because he could never quite leave a silence alone: "Ser Warrant?"

"Wat."

"When you treat with them. Will you swear?"

"If I must."

The boy was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its brightness and found something underneath it, something the knight had not known was there, and was sorry to find. "My mother said a guarantor's oath is a hard thing to carry. She said her grandfather was one, before Marrenhold had a proper court, and that he swore too easily when he was young and it — she said it wore him down. Like water on a step." He looked over, earnest and afraid of his own earnestness. "You never swear easily. I've watched. You'd rather do a thing three times over than swear it once."

The knight looked at the boy for a while. The horses' breath smoked in the cold. Above the pass the stars were very clear and very far, the way they only were in the mountains, as if the world had been lifted up nearer to something and had forgotten to feel honored by it.

"Your mother is wise," he said finally. "Yes. I would rather do a thing than swear it. A deed you can undo, or mend, or answer for. An oath, once sealed, is not yours anymore. It has you. And a man ought to know precisely what he is handing over before he hands it over, because he will not get it back." He gathered his own reins. "Remember that, when it is your turn to wear the grey. Swear last. Swear least. And never, ever swear a thing you have not already decided to become."

"I'll remember," said Wat, in the tone of a boy filing away a thing he is certain he already understands, which is to say the tone of a boy who has understood nothing and will, in time, understand it entirely and at great cost. The knight did not correct him. There are lessons that can only be taught by the thing itself.

They rode out through the low gate as the sky went from black to the deep bruised grey that passed for dawn in the pass, and Marrenhold fell away behind them, stone upon stone upon stone, small and stubborn and beloved, a kingdom that had survived four hundred years by being too honest to be worth the trouble of a lie.

---

The land changed slowly, and then all at once.

For two days the road ran down out of the high pass through country the knight knew — terraced barley, stone-walled folds of mountain sheep, the little grey villages that clung to the switchbacks and waved at a passing guarantor because a guarantor meant the crown remembered they existed. The people fed them and would not take coin, and asked, in the roundabout way of frightened people, whether the thing on the mesa was going to be dealt with. The knight told them the truth, which was that he was going to look at it, and they took more comfort from that than the words deserved, because he was Ser Warrant and his looking at a thing was the beginning of the thing being set right.

He wondered, some of those miles, what it did to a man to be believed in like that. Nothing good, he suspected. Belief was a weight like any other. He had watched it bend better men than himself.

On the third day the villages thinned, and the walls fell into disrepair, and the barley gave way to rough grazing, and then the grazing gave way to something the knight had no word for at first.

It began as a smell. The wind out of the east carried it up the road to meet them, and the grey mare tossed her head and did not want to walk into it — a dry smell, a burned smell, though nothing burned, the smell of a hearth three days cold, of a thing that had once been alive and was now only the shape of having been. Wat pulled his scarf up over his nose. The knight left his down. It was his craft to smell a thing fully before he named it.

Then they came over a low rise, and he saw it.

The land ahead had died. Not scorched — scorching leaves black, and there is a violence in it that the eye can understand. This was quieter and far worse. The grass had gone the colour of old bone and lay flat, as if it had lain down to sleep and simply not risen. The few trees stood grey and leafless in a season that had no business stripping them, their bark peeling away in long pale curls like skin. The soil between showed through in patches, and the patches were the wrong colour, a fine ashen grey that the wind lifted and carried and laid down again in drifts against the dead stalks, so that the whole ruined plain seemed to smoke faintly without fire. Where a stream had been, there was a bed of grey mud, cracked into plates. Nothing moved in all of it. No bird, no hare, no beetle. The knight had ridden through battlefields the day after, and they had been more alive than this.

"Ser," Wat said, and his voice had gone small. "Ser, what — what did this?"

"Lingering," said the knight, though he had not entirely known the word until he said it. He sat his horse at the edge of the dying and made himself look at it fully, the whole grey sweep of it, the way he had made himself look at every hard thing since he was a child who had owned nothing, not even the right to look away. Somewhere in that waste, a season ago, a people had stopped. And the earth, for reasons the tales gave one way and the truth would surely give another, had begun to die rather than hold them.

He had come expecting monsters. He was honest enough, even now, to admit that. He had ridden three days composing in his mind the shape of the thing he would move along — some brute horde, some plague of scaled devils squatting on good Marrenhold ground out of malice or appetite. It was easier to move a thing you had already decided to despise.

But there was no malice in what lay before him. There was only ruin, and the terrible patience of ruin, and — he could see it now, far off, where the dead plain rose at last into a great flat-topped hill of red stone standing alone against the mountains — there was smoke. Real smoke this time, thin and blue and rising, the smoke of cook-fires, of a camp, of people making their supper at the far edge of the land they were killing simply by needing somewhere to be.

The Kessith mesa. And on it, the thing he had been sent to move.

"There," the knight said, and pointed, and felt something he did not yet have a name for and would not have a name for until it was far too late to matter. "That is where we are going."

Wat looked at the smoke, and at the grey drifting waste between them and it, and back at the knight, and for once in his young loud life he said nothing at all.

They rode down into the dying land as the light failed, and behind them, unnoticed, the ash closed softly over the print of the horses' hooves, as though to say that they, too, had never been there; as though the ground had already begun the work of forgetting them.
