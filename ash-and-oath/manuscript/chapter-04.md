# Chapter 4: The Bargain

Morning came grey and without much conviction, the sun a pale coin behind the ash-haze that hung always over the dying plain, and the knight woke on the cold stone at the camp's edge to find that he had, against his intention, slept.

He lay still a moment and took the measure of it. He had not meant to sleep — had meant to wake through the night by turns with Wat, a hostage's habit, a soldier's — and instead he had gone down into a black dreamless rest the moment he lay on the rock, deeper than he had slept in months, and woken clear and whole and rested in a way he could not remember being since he was young enough not to notice being it. It unsettled him more than a bad night would have. A man did not sleep like that among enemies. His body, wiser or more foolish than his caution, had decided somewhere in the dark that it was not among enemies, and had not asked his leave, and the knight lay on the cold stone and distrusted his own ease and could not, try as he might, manufacture the wariness that ought to have come with waking a prisoner in a monster-camp. There was no wariness in him. There was only the grey morning, and the smell of someone's cook-fire, and the low ordinary sounds of a people beginning a day.

Wat was already up, of course, sitting cross-legged a careful arm's length away and trying not to gawk at a camp coming awake around him and failing at it wholesale, his young face full of a wonder he had not yet learned to be ashamed of. A girl with pale grey scales had brought them water without being asked and set it down and gone, and the boy kept looking at the cup as though it might be a trick, and looking at the camp, and looking at the knight to see whether he was permitted to find any of it as strange and as unfrightening as he plainly did.

"They gave us water," Wat said, low.

"They gave us water," the knight agreed.

"Monsters don't give you water, Ser."

"No," said the knight. "They don't." He let the boy sit with that. It was a lesson better arrived at than delivered.

They brought him to the old man after the fold had eaten, which the knight marked as its own small courtesy: they had fed their own first, and fed the horses, and only then sent for the crown's man — and had sent food to him as well, flatbread still warm and a hard salt cheese, plain and enough and unpoisoned, which he knew because he watched the woman who brought it tear the same loaf and eat of it before she set it down, a gesture so old and so deliberate that it could only be a message. *We do not murder guests. Note that we have told you so.* A people who fed a hostage before they haggled with him, and troubled to prove the food safe, were telling him a great deal about themselves, if he cared to hear it. He cared to hear everything. It was his craft.

The old man sat where he had sat the night before, in daylight no less weary but somehow more solid, the banked red-gold of him steady in the flat morning. The earth-bright woman stood at his shoulder again. Soraya. The knight had the name now, filed and kept, and did not use it, because a name was a key and he did not hand his own to strangers and would not presume to turn theirs.

"You slept," the old man said, with the ghost of that dry humour. "Good. A man decides things badly on no sleep, and we have a matter to decide."

"Name your price," the knight said. "You said there was one. I have crossed the dark and slept on your stone and eaten your bread. I would hear it."

"Passage," the old man said.

The knight waited. He had learned the value of the silence after a word, the room it gave a man to fill.

"We will go," the old man went on. "We always go; you know that much by now. But going is not free, guarantor, and that is the part your kingdom has never once troubled to understand, because it costs your kingdom nothing to stay. It is a long road from the Kessith frontier to any ground far enough that we might rest a season before we must move again, and every kingdom and every duchy and every free town between here and there will want us gone the moment we appear, and each will send us on a little poorer than the last. Wagons break on such a road. Cattle sicken. Stores run through, and no one will sell to us on the way, because a people who leave ash behind them do not get to buy on credit anywhere in the world; we pay in coin, always, up front, always, and we are nearly out of coin. That is why we have wintered on this cursed mesa killing your king's fields — not out of malice, not to spite him, but because we had run out of the road. We stopped here because we could not afford to go on." He lifted his gold eyes. "You want us off your frontier before the wells turn. Then your king must buy us the road. Grain. Draft-beasts to replace the ones we will lose. Coin enough to see us through the lands beyond his own, where his law does not run and his good name does not feed us. Fund the leaving, and it will be done, and done gladly, and no well will turn. That is the price. It is not in blood. It is only in the one coin your kind finds hardest to spend on people it has already decided are not its own."

It was, the knight thought, an entirely reasonable price, and he said so, and watched the saying land on the old man as an unexpected weight.

"It is reasonable," the knight said. "It is less than a season of soldiers on this frontier would cost the treasury, and a very great deal less than a war over poisoned wells, and my king is a man who can do that arithmetic and will. He will pay it. I will tell him he should, and he listens to me on such matters, which is the one power my office carries that is worth carrying." He paused, because here the ground grew difficult, and a man ought to walk difficult ground slowly. "But I did not ride here with a treasury at my back. I have a squire, two horses, and my word. The grain and the beasts and the coin are three days behind me in Marrenhold, and someone must go and fetch them, and that will take time — a fortnight, if the roads are kind. More, if they are not."

Something moved at the old man's shoulder. The woman had gone very still, and the knight understood, watching her, that they had come to the crux of it — the place where these bargains always died.

"So." The old man's voice did not change, but the humour had gone out of it, and what came into its place was older and harder and very quiet. "You ask us to strike camp on your promise. To break out the wagons and load them and stand our people on the open road, in the cold, with the little ones and the old ones and the sick, on the strength of a crown's word that a fortnight hence the grain will surely come." He shook his heavy head, slow and certain. "It has been promised before, guarantor. Not this grain, not this king — but a road paid for, a passage funded, a kindness that would surely come if only we would go first and trust it. We went first. We are very good at going first; it is the one skill our whole history has taught us. And the kindness did not come, and we were caught in the open with nothing, and we buried what the trusting cost us in ground we do not even have the names of anymore." He spread his scarred hands, and for a moment the ash under his eyes was not weariness but something closer to the bottom of grief. "A promise is air. You are asking my people to eat air and to walk on it. I will not do it. I would sooner sit here and watch the mesa turn to sand beneath us, because at least the sand is honest, and the sand, when it takes my people, will not first have taught them to hope."

And there it was. The knight had known it was coming since the word *passage* — had felt the shape of it forming, the old dead end where the crown's good intention met the plain fact that the crown had lied before, in other mouths, and that a promise was worth exactly as much as the last promise had been, which was nothing. This was the moment the bargain broke. He had seen it break a dozen times, in a dozen rooms, and he had learned that there was only one way through it, and that the way was not made of words.

He had also, twice in eleven years, seen it not break. And he knew to the ounce what it cost to make it hold.

"Then I will not ask you to eat air," the knight said. "I will give you something with weight in it. I will stay."

The old man went still. The woman's gold eyes came to the knight's face and fixed there, and did not leave it.

"I am a guarantor of Marrenhold," the knight said, and now he chose the words with great care, because these were words that would bind, and a man ought to know precisely what he is handing over before he hands it over. "Do you understand what that means, or only the name of it? When I give my word as a guarantor, it is not a promise. It is sworn in the faith of my god, and sealed, and it holds me the way — I think — your curse holds you: not by my honour, which could fail, but in my flesh, which cannot lie its way free. If I swear that the grain will come, and it does not come, I am not merely shamed. I am unmade. The office stripped, the standing stripped, the name I was given taken back, everything the oath ever made of me undone down to the root, until there is nothing left standing but the nothing I was born. My king knows this. He does not spend his guarantors on lies, because a spent guarantor is a public and a permanent proof that the crown's word is false, and the crown's word is the only wall this small kingdom has ever had, and he would sooner burn his own treasury than crack it." He held the old man's gaze. "So I will swear it. I will swear that the price will come, on the binding, with my unmaking as the stake. And I will stay here, in your camp, in your hand, until it does. Send my squire to fetch the grain. Keep me against his return. If it fails, you will have a forsworn man to do with as you please — and the wider world will have watched Marrenhold break faith with a people it drove out, which my king would die before he permitted. My body for your trust. My unmaking against your dead. That is what I have to set on the ground beside your fear, and whatever else it is, it is not air."

The silence after that was a different silence than the ones before it. Even the camp seemed to have gone quiet around them, though the knight knew that was only his own attention narrowing; the world always went quiet when a man laid his whole self down on a table and waited to see if it would be taken.

The old man looked at him for a long, long while. Something worked behind the banked gold of his eyes, something the knight could not read and did not try to — grief, perhaps, or memory, or the private arithmetic of a man who had staked everything on a broken oath once himself and knew to the last grain what such a stake weighed and what it cost to lose it. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet in a wholly different way than before, and there was in it, for the first time, something that was almost — not warmth. Recognition. The look one keeper of faith gives another across a fire, each seeing in the other the shape of his own chain.

"You would truly do that," the old man said. It was not a question. "Not as a gambit, not as a clever throw of the bones. You have already decided to become it, if it comes to that. I can see it on you — the way a man carries what he has already chosen to die for, if he must. It sits differently on the shoulders than a bluff does." He was quiet a moment more, and his gaze moved over the knight's face with a new and searching attention, as though he were reading something written there in a hand he half remembered. "I have known one other kind of creature that binds itself so — that puts its own unmaking on the table and means it, and pays when the debt is called. I did not think to meet it again wearing a crown's grey, on the far end of my life, sent to move me along." He inclined his head at last, the slow deep nod of a man conceding not a point but a person. "Send your squire. You will stay. We will not break camp until the grain is in our hands, and if it comes as you have sworn, we will go, and there will be no ash on your king's conscience and no blood on mine. And if it does not come—" the ghost of the old dry humour, and beneath it something colder and very tired— "then we will both of us learn what your oath was worth. Which is, I have found, the only knowledge in this world that is ever worth the price of the learning."

He put out his hand. The knight took it. The old man's grip was dry and immensely strong, a banked strength like the banked colour of him, and warm — warmer than a hand had any right to be in that grey cold, as though somewhere far down under the ash the fire still burned, and would burn, the knight thought, long after everything nearer the surface had gone to grey.

He told Wat at the horses, in the grey noon, with the fold moving about its business at a courteous distance that was not quite far enough to be private.

The boy took it badly, as the knight had known he would.

"Leave you here." Wat's voice cracked on it, and he was furious with the crack, the knight could see, furious to be sixteen in a moment that wanted a man. "Alone. In their camp. As a — Ser, as a *hostage*, that is what it is, whatever fine word you dress it in, and you want me to just ride away and leave you in it—"

"Yes."

"What if the grain's held up? What if the treasury balks, or the roads flood, or the steward decides a guarantor's word isn't enough and wants it in triplicate under three seals — a fortnight, you said, but what if it's a month, what if it's two, what if—" He stopped. His jaw worked. He was trying so hard to be a man about it that the knight could not have loved him more if he had been his own son, which he was not, which no one was, which was rather the whole of the knight's life laid bare in a single grey noon. "What if it doesn't come, Ser. What then. What happens to you then."

"Then it doesn't come," the knight said, "and you will not be here, which is the entire reason I am sending you and not one of theirs. Listen to me, Wat." He put his hand on the boy's shoulder, which he did rarely, and felt the boy go still under it. "You will ride to Marrenhold. You will go to the king — to the king himself, not the steward, not the treasury clerk, the king — and you will tell him exactly what I have sworn and exactly what stands upon it, and he will move the grain, because he understands to the ounce what I have put on that table, and because he told me to come home, and he does not say such words lightly and he does not spend his men. You will bring the grain back. You will not ride so hard that you break a horse or your neck in a ditch, because a dead squire buys me nothing and would grieve the one man in that city who can least afford more grief. You will do this well, and steadily, and right — the way you do everything, when you forget to be afraid. Can you do that?"

The boy's eyes were bright and wet and he was not going to let them spill, and the knight loved him, plainly and privately, the way he loved almost nothing, and did not say so, because saying so would have made it a farewell, and it was not going to be a farewell.

"I can do that," Wat said.

"I know you can. It is why it is you and not another." The knight took his hand from the boy's shoulder before the boy could feel it wanting to stay. "Go now, while the light holds. And Wat—" The boy turned back, hope and dread at war on his open face. "Say nothing in Marrenhold of how they live up here. Not to the court, not in the taverns, not to the girl in the lower town, not to anyone who did not ride up this mesa and see it with his own eyes. They have been made into monsters by people who never once looked at them, and I will not have you feeding the tale, because the tale is half of what keeps a sword ready over their heads. When they ask you what the Emberkin are, you will say: a people. Tired, and poor, and keeping their word better than the men sent to move them. That is the truth, and it is the whole of the truth, and it is the one charge I am ordering you to carry home unbroken."

Wat looked at him a moment, and something in the boy's face shifted — a small growing, the first of many the road would ask of him. "A people," he repeated, slowly, tasting the weight of it against everything he had been raised to believe. "Yes, Ser. A people."

Then he mounted, and gathered his reins, and did not trust his voice for more, and rode down off the mesa into the grey, leading the second horse behind him, growing small against the dead plain and smaller, until the ash-haze took him and there was only the grey and the wind and the low continual settling of a land coming apart.

The knight watched until there was nothing left to watch. Then he turned back to the camp — to the wagons and the fires and the wary scaled faces that were, for a fortnight or a month or however long the grain took, to be the whole of his world; to the old man who had recognized him, and the earth-bright woman who had not stopped watching him since the dark on the path, and who was watching him still from the shadow of a wagon now, her gold eyes unreadable, her arms folded, giving nothing — and he walked back up into it, a guarantor of Marrenhold, sworn and staked and entirely alone, to begin the wait that would, in the end, take from him every last gift the oath had ever given, and one thing besides that the oath had never thought to name.

He did not know that yet. He knew only that he had kept the blood off the ground, which was his craft, and that the sleeping had been easy, which should have warned him, and did not.
