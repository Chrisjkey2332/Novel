# Chapter 4: The Bargain

Morning came grey and without much conviction, the sun a pale coin behind the ash-haze that hung always over the dying plain, and the knight woke on the cold stone at the camp's edge to find that he had, against his intention, slept.

He lay still a moment and took the measure of it. He had not meant to sleep — had meant to wake through the night by turns with Wat, a hostage's habit, a soldier's — and instead he had gone down into a black dreamless rest the moment he lay on the rock, deeper than he had slept in months, and woken clear. It unsettled him more than a bad night would have. A man did not sleep like that among enemies. His body, wiser than his caution, had decided somewhere in the dark that it was not among enemies, and had not asked his leave.

Wat was already up, of course, sitting cross-legged and trying not to gawk at a camp coming awake around him and failing at it, his young face full of a wonder he had not yet learned to be ashamed of. The knight let him have the wonder a moment longer before he rose.

They brought him to the old man after the fold had eaten, which the knight marked as its own small courtesy: they had fed their own first, and fed the horses, and only then sent for the crown's man, and had sent food to him too, flatbread and a hard salt cheese, plain and enough. A people who fed a hostage before they haggled with him were telling him something about themselves, if he cared to hear it. He cared to hear everything. It was his craft.

The old man sat where he had sat the night before, in daylight no less weary but somehow more solid, the banked red-gold of him steady in the flat morning. The earth-bright woman stood at his shoulder again. Soraya. The knight had the name now, filed and kept, and did not use it, because a name was a key and he did not hand his own to strangers and would not presume to turn theirs.

"You slept," the old man said, with the ghost of that dry humour. "Good. A man decides things badly on no sleep, and we have a thing to decide."

"Name your price," the knight said. "You said there was one. I have crossed the dark and slept on your stone and eaten your bread. I would hear it."

"Passage," the old man said.

The knight waited. He had learned the value of the silence after a word, the room it gave a man to fill.

"We will go," the old man went on. "We always go; you know that by now. But going is not free, guarantor, and that is the part your kingdom has never once troubled to understand. It is a long road from the Kessith frontier to any ground far enough that we might rest a season before we must move again, and every kingdom and every duchy and every free town between here and there will want us gone the moment we appear, and we will arrive at each of them poorer than we left the last. We have wintered on this mesa because we had run out of the means to go further. Wagons break. Cattle die. Stores run through. A people who leave ash behind them do not get to buy on credit anywhere in the world." He lifted his gold eyes. "You want us off your king's frontier before the wells turn. Then your king must pay for the road that takes us there. Grain. Draft-beasts. Coin enough to buy passage through the lands beyond his own, where his law does not run and his good name does not feed us. Fund the leaving, and it will be done, and done gladly, and no wells will turn. That is the price. It is not in blood. It is only in the one currency your kind finds hard to spend on people it has already decided are not its own."

It was, the knight thought, an entirely reasonable price, and he said so, and watched that land on the old man as an unexpected weight.

"It is reasonable," the knight said. "It is less than a season of soldiers on this frontier would cost the treasury, and far less than a war over poisoned wells. My king will pay it, and I will tell him he should, and he listens to me on such matters." He paused, because here the ground grew difficult, and a man ought to walk difficult ground slowly. "But I did not ride here with a treasury at my back. I have a squire, two horses, and my word. The grain and the beasts and the coin are three days behind me in Marrenhold, and someone must go and fetch them, and that will take time — a fortnight, if the roads are kind. Perhaps more."

Something moved at the old man's shoulder. The woman had gone very still, and the knight understood, watching her, that they had come to the crux of it, the place where these bargains always died.

"So." The old man's voice did not change, but the humour had gone out of it. "You ask us to strike camp on your promise. To break the wagons out and load them and stand our people on the open road, in the cold, with the little ones, on the strength of a crown's word that a fortnight from now the grain will surely come." He shook his heavy head, slow and old and certain. "It has been promised before, guarantor. Not this grain, not this king — but a road paid for, a passage funded, a kindness that would surely come if only we would go first and trust it. We went first. The kindness did not come. We buried what the going cost us in ground we do not even have the names of anymore." He spread his scarred hands. "A promise is air. You are asking my people to eat air and walk on it. I will not do it. I would sooner sit here and watch the mesa turn to sand under us, because at least the sand is honest."

And there it was. The knight had known it was coming since the word *passage* — had felt the shape of it forming, the old dead end where the crown's good intention met the plain fact that the crown had lied before, in other mouths, and that a promise was worth exactly as much as the last promise had been, which was nothing. This was the moment the bargain broke. He had seen it break a dozen times, in a dozen rooms.

He had also, twice, seen it not break. And he knew what it cost to make it hold.

"Then I will not ask you to eat air," the knight said. "I will give you something with weight in it. I will stay."

The old man went still. The woman's gold eyes came to the knight's face and fixed there.

"I am a guarantor of Marrenhold," the knight said, and now he chose the words with great care, because these were words that would bind, and a man ought to know precisely what he is handing over before he hands it over. "Do you understand what that means, or only the name of it? When I give my word as a guarantor, it is not a promise. It is sworn in the faith of my god, and sealed, and it holds me the way your curse holds you — not by my honor, which could fail, but in my flesh, which cannot lie its way free. If I swear that the grain will come, and it does not come, I am not merely shamed. I am unmade — the office stripped, the standing stripped, everything the oath ever gave me taken back down to the root. My king knows this. He does not spend his guarantors on lies, because a spent guarantor is a public and permanent proof that the crown's word is false, and the crown's word is the only wall this small kingdom has ever had." He held the old man's gaze. "So I will swear it. I will swear the price will come, on the binding, with my unmaking as the stake. And I will stay here, in your camp, in your hand, until it does. Send my squire to fetch it. Keep me against his return. If the grain fails, you will have a forsworn man to do with as you please — and the world will have watched Marrenhold break faith with a people it drove out, which my king would sooner die than permit. My body for your trust. My unmaking against your dead. That is what I have to put on the ground beside your fear, and it is not air."

The silence after that was a different silence than the ones before it.

The old man looked at him for a long, long while. Something worked behind the banked gold of his eyes, something the knight could not read and did not try to — grief, perhaps, or memory, or the private arithmetic of a man who had staked everything on a broken oath once himself and knew to the ounce what such a stake weighed. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet, and there was in it, for the first time, something that was almost — not warmth. Recognition. The look one keeper of faith gives another across a fire, each seeing in the other the shape of his own chain.

"You would truly do that," the old man said. "Not as a gambit. You have already decided to become it, if it comes to that. I can see it on you." He was quiet a moment more. "I have known one other kind of creature that binds itself so — that puts its own unmaking on the table and means it. I did not expect to meet it wearing a crown's grey." He inclined his head, at last, the slow deep nod of a man conceding not a point but a person. "Send your squire. You will stay. We will not break camp until the grain is in our hands, and if it comes as you swear, we will go, and there will be no ash on your king's conscience and no blood on mine. And if it does not come—" the ghost of the old dry humour, and under it something colder— "then we will both of us learn what your oath is worth. Which is, I have found, the only thing in this world ever worth the learning."

He told Wat at the horses, in the grey noon, with the fold moving about its business at a courteous distance that was not quite far enough to be private.

The boy took it badly, as the knight had known he would.

"Leave you here." Wat's voice cracked on it, and he was furious with the crack, the knight could see, furious to be sixteen in a moment that wanted a man. "Alone. In their camp. As a — Ser, as a *hostage*, that's what it is, whatever pretty word you put on it, and you want me to just ride away and leave you—"

"Yes."

"What if the grain's held up? What if the treasury balks, or the roads flood, or — a fortnight, you said, but what if it's a month, what if it's—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "What if it doesn't come, Ser. What then."

"Then it doesn't come," the knight said, "and you will not be here, which is the entire reason I am sending you and not keeping you. Listen to me, Wat." He put his hand on the boy's shoulder, which he did rarely, and felt the boy go still under it. "You will ride to Marrenhold. You will go to the king — to the king himself, not the steward, not the treasury clerk, the king — and you will tell him exactly what I have sworn and exactly what stands on it, and he will move the grain, because he understands to the ounce what I have put on the table and he does not spend his men. You will bring it back. You will not hurry so badly that you break a horse or your neck, because a dead squire in a ditch buys me nothing. You will do this well, and slowly, and right, the way you do everything when you forget to be afraid. Can you do that?"

The boy's eyes were bright and wet and he was not going to let them spill, and the knight loved him, plainly and privately, the way he loved almost nothing, and did not say so, because saying so would have made it a farewell and it was not going to be a farewell.

"I can do that," Wat said.

"I know you can. It is why it is you." The knight took his hand from the boy's shoulder. "Go now, while the light holds. And Wat—" The boy turned back, hope and dread at war on his open face. "Say nothing in Marrenhold of how they live up here. Not to the court, not in the taverns, not to anyone who did not ride up this mesa with his own eyes. They have been made into monsters by people who never once looked at them, and I will not have you feeding the tale. When they ask what the Emberkin are, you will say: a people. Tired, and poor, and keeping their word. That is the truth, and it is the whole of it, and it is the one thing I am ordering you to carry home."

Wat looked at him a moment, and something in the boy's face shifted — a small growing, the first of many the road would ask of him. "A people," he repeated. "Yes, Ser."

Then he mounted, and gathered his reins, and did not trust his voice for more, and rode down off the mesa into the grey, leading the second horse, growing small against the dead plain and smaller, until the ash-haze took him.

The knight watched until there was nothing left to watch. Then he turned back to the camp — to the wagons and the fires and the wary scaled faces that were, for a fortnight or a month or however long the grain took, to be his whole world; to the old man who had recognized him and the earth-bright woman who had not stopped watching him since the dark on the path — and he walked back up into it, a guarantor of Marrenhold, sworn and staked and entirely alone, to begin the wait that would, in the end, take everything from him.

He did not know that yet. He knew only that he had kept the blood off the ground, which was his craft, and that the sleeping had been easy, which should have warned him, and did not.
