# Chapter 5: The Guarantor

By the time the sun had cleared the ash-haze the whole fold knew, and by midday the whole fold had an opinion, and Soraya spent the morning walking through the low steady weather of their fear the way she walked through everything, taking it into her and setting none of it down.

They were afraid. Of course they were afraid. A human was to live among them — not pass through, not stand at the edge of the fire for a night and be gone, but *stay*: sleep inside the ring of the wagons, eat their bread, walk their camp, day upon day, for as long as it took a boy to ride to a kingdom and back with a promise made solid. It had not happened in living memory. It had not happened, so far as the old songs told, ever. And her people had learned across three hundred years exactly what it meant when the crowns took an interest in them, and there was not one soul in the camp whose fear was foolish, and that was the worst of it: she could not tell any of them they were wrong to be afraid, because they were not.

She felt the fear the way she felt the ground — through the soles, through the skin, a low continual pressure — and she moved through it doing what the heir did, which was to be seen being unafraid so that others could borrow the unafraid from her, a currency she did not herself possess in the amounts she spent. She stopped where she was needed to be stopped. She let the anxious ones say their piece and did not hurry them. She was the still point they steadied themselves against, and being the still point was exhausting in a way that had no name and left no mark and could not be set down, ever, because the moment the heir set it down the whole trembling fold would feel the tremor and fall.

Nima's mother stopped her by the water-wagon, a child on her hip and two more at her skirts, and did not quite ask the question, because you did not quite ask the heir a question you did not want answered. "He will sleep near the children," the woman said, which was not a question. "The human."

"He will sleep where I put him," Soraya said, "and I will put him where I can see him, and I can see a great deal." She reached out — she did not plan it; her hand simply went, the way it always did toward the small ones — and touched the littlest child's cheek, the soft warm scaleless cheek of a baby too young yet for the colour to have come in, and felt the mother's fear ease by a fraction under the touch, because the heir had touched her child and not flinched from the trouble, and that was a kind of promise too, wordless, the only kind Soraya trusted herself to make. "Nothing will happen to them that I do not allow. You have my word, which you have never once had reason to doubt. Go on."

The woman went on. And behind her came others, through the long morning, in the roundabout way of frightened people who did not want to seem to be asking: old Petras, who wanted to know whether the human's horse would be eating the fodder they could not spare, and was told it would not, that the boy had taken it; a knot of the younger mothers who said nothing and only came near her and stood, the way sheep drift toward the one dog they have decided is theirs; a grey-scaled man who said, flatly, that if the human touched one of the little ones he would deal with it himself, curse or no curse, and Soraya had looked at him and seen the terror under the threat and said only, gently, "You will not have to. But I am glad the little ones have you," and watched the threat go out of him and leave the terror bare, and had touched his arm, and moved on, spent another coin, kept walking.

That was the whole of it, that morning, and it was enough, and it left her feeling scraped thin in a way she did not examine, because comfort was something you gave out of your own store and hers was not large and the fold was two hundred souls deep in fear and she was one woman with two hands and a name.

Vesh, at least, was not afraid — or was afraid and had decided, the way she decided everything, in favour of kindness. The old woman caught Soraya at the midday fire and pressed a cup of something hot into her hands unasked, which was Vesh's way of ordering a person to stop for the length of a cup, and said, watching the camp, "They will settle. They always settle. We are very practiced at being afraid of something and living beside it anyway; it is half of what we are." She sipped. "I mean to feed him, you know. The human. Tonight or tomorrow. Do not make your face at me, child, I saw it — I mean to feed him, because a frightened camp is a cruel camp, and the fastest way to gentle a fear is to make what is feared sit down and eat where everyone can watch it be only a man. You keep him safe your way. I will keep us kind mine. Between us we may get through the season without anyone doing something we would all have to grieve." And she had patted Soraya's cheek with one soft blue hand and gone back to her mending, and Soraya had stood holding the warm cup and loved her, helplessly, the way she was always loving people helplessly, which was the great ungovernable weakness under all her discipline and the one she guarded most.

---

Her father sent for her in the afternoon, and told her what she already knew he would.

"You will keep him."

"Set a watch," she said, and hated that the words came out in Ravan's shape, because Ravan had said the same to her at the picket an hour before, bright-scaled and afraid for her under his temper, and she had told him *careful* in the soft voice that ended things and he had gone, and the ending of him sat in her still, one more unadded sum. "I have a hundred better uses for my hours than nursing a crown's man—"

"You will keep him," her father said again, gently, and there was no arguing with the gentleness; she could have argued with an order. "Not because I distrust him. Because I do trust him, more than I have trusted a crown's man in three hundred years, and a trust that large frightens me more than any danger I can name, and I want it watched by the one pair of eyes in this camp I trust as much. That is the first reason." He was quiet a moment, and the banked red-gold of him dimmed and steadied, the way it did before he said something that cost him. "The second reason I will say once, and you will not like it, and you will do as I ask regardless, because you love me and because I am right. You are the heir. One day — not soon, I am not so tired as that yet, but one day — you will lead this fold down the road alone, and you will treat with men like him, and worse than him, and you will have to know how to sit across a fire from a crown and give it exactly as much of yourself as the bargain needs and not one grain more. That is a hard art. It cannot be taught in the safe years. It can only be learned on a living man, at real stakes — and here is a living man, at real stakes, sworn into our hand for a season. I am giving you the best teacher the road has offered in a lifetime. Learn him. Learn how to stand near an honest enemy and stay whole. You will need it when I am ash."

*When I am ash.* He said it lightly, the way he said everything that could not be borne, as though he were remarking on the weather, and she felt it go into her like cold water and did not let it show, because he had taught her not to let it show, because he had taught her everything she knew about not letting anything show, and she understood, sitting there in the failing light, that this was the real lesson under the lesson: he was old, and tired, and preparing her, and had found in a doomed crown's man a whetstone for the blade he would have to leave behind. And she could refuse him nothing. He knew it. He had always known it, and had used it as sparingly as a good man uses such a power, which was why the rare times he spent it landed so hard.

"You are afraid of him for me," she said. It was not quite a question. "Vesh, Ravan, you — you have all found a way to warn me of him in a single day. What is it you think I am going to do?"

Her father looked at her for a long moment, and did not answer the question she had asked, which was its own answer.

"I think," he said at last, "that you have wanted a home your whole life, and buried the wanting so deep you have half forgotten it is there, and that this man carries a home in him without knowing it — not a place, he has no place, but the shape of one, the way a lamp carries light it did not make. And I think you will warm your hands at it before you remember you are not allowed near a fire." He reached out and cupped her jaw in one enormous warm scarred hand, the way he had when she was small, and looked at her with the banked gold gone soft. "Keep him, Soraya. Learn him. And keep your own counsel about the rest, because I cannot forbid a heart anything — I learned that the hardest way a man can learn it, long ago — and I would not shame us both by trying."

She did not ask him what he meant by *the hardest way*. She never did. It was one of the doors in her father she had learned not to open, because what was behind it was grief, and his grief was the one weight in all the world she could not lift for him, and could only, by not looking, help him carry.

---

She found the human at the cold edge of the camp where she had left him, sitting with his back to a wagon-wheel and his eyes half-closed and his hands loose and open on his knees, so still that a stranger might have taken him for asleep. She knew he was not. She could feel his attention even shut-eyed — a quiet unblinking awareness that took in the camp around him the way she took in the ground, patiently, missing nothing.

"You are to be mine," she said, without softening it. "The keeping of you. My father's word. You go where I permit and nowhere else, you speak to whom I allow, and if you are wise you will make yourself as small and as dull as you can manage, because the fold is frightened of you, and a frightened people set to watch over children is dangerous, and I would rather not spend the season keeping you alive from my own." She folded her arms. "Do you understand the terms?"

The human opened his eyes. They found her, and stayed, without heat and without fear and without that awful crown's-man reasonableness she was braced against — only that quiet unhurried attention, as though she had said something worth the whole of his care.

"I understand them," he said. "And I will keep them, gladly, and I will tell you why, so you need not waste your watching on my motives. I am in your camp on my own oath, which is worth my life. Every hour I make your people less afraid is an hour I am safer, and an hour the bargain holds, and an hour nearer taking you all off ground that is killing you and my king's people both. Our interests are the same, guarantor's-keeper. I could not harm your fold if I wished to, and I do not wish to. Set me any task that makes me useful rather than watched. I would rather be a pair of hands than a danger." He paused, and something moved, very briefly, under the shut quiet of him, and was gone. "I have spent my whole life being watched. I find I would rather be spent."

It was not what she had braced for. She had braced for smoothness, the practiced humility of a man managing her, and this was not that; it was too plain, too unguarded in one small place, a single window in a dark house left carelessly uncurtained. And she had looked through it before she could stop herself and seen — she did not know what she had seen. Something tired. Something that had been alone a very long time and had stopped expecting to be otherwise. Something that, God help her, she recognized, because she carried its twin.

"I did not ask for your reasons," she said, more roughly than she meant, because the roughness was a wall and she wanted a wall.

"No," the human agreed. "You did not. I gave them anyway. It is a habit of my office — I tell the truth whether or not it is wanted, because a man who tells the truth only when it is welcome is not a truthful man, only a comfortable one." The ghost of something that was not a smile. "You will find it tiresome. Most people do."

And Soraya, who had come to set the terms of a keeping and raise a wall and be done, stood in the grey afternoon with her arms folded and her carefully cold terms all delivered, and felt the wall she had built not fall — she would not let it fall — but shift, very slightly, on its foundation, the way earth shifts before a slower, deeper movement that has not yet arrived.

She thought of her father's soft voice. *You will warm your hands at it before you remember you are not allowed near a fire.* She thought of Ravan's, and Vesh's, and the grey-scaled man's bare terror, all of them warning her of the same danger from a hundred directions, as though the whole fold could see a weather coming that she alone was forbidden to name.

"Rest tonight," she told the human, because she had to say something and could not say any of that. "Tomorrow you will work. My people will watch you be only a man. See that you are only a man." And she turned, and left him there, and walked back into her camp with her heart going in a way she did not permit and could not, quite, prevent.

The watch had begun. She had thought it would be the simplest duty her father had ever set her. She was already, in the wordless place, afraid it would be the hardest — and she did not let herself know why — and the ground under her bare feet went on dying, patiently, and told no one, and kept, as it always kept, her secret with her.
